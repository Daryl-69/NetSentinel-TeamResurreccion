NetSentinel V2 · escalation-routing trial

Can a cheap Sentry route well enough to keep the Inspector's ceiling?

Three ways of building the always-on layer, raced against each other at equal escalation budget. One of them is the architecture already in the design doc. After a bug fix, it came first.

3 seeds 300 hosts × 24 days temporal train→val→test thresholds on validation only CPU, ~2 min/seed + shift test: 2 worlds

What came out

Withdrawn

A bug reversed this result — the earlier finding is retracted

The edge aggregator's max-pool guarded on a clamped denominator, so a -1e9 masking sentinel leaked into the encoder on every empty window — about half of them. First-epoch loss ran to 3.7 × 1012. It converged anyway, which is why nothing downstream looked wrong. Because -1e9 made empty windows trivially separable, the Mahalanobis router harvested that artifact as free signal and appeared to win. Fixed and re-run: the original design wins instead.

Corrected

Distilling the whole detector beats distilling only the encoder

Router A — the design already in the V2 document — scores 0.876 against the Inspector's decision, ahead of the deferral head at 0.850 and the Mahalanobis baseline at 0.818. The distillation-transfer argument predicted the opposite. At this dimensionality it does not hold, and B's variance is seven times A's, which is the covariance-estimation instability the hardening note flagged: 4,000 commissioning windows is too thin for a 32-dimensional per-host covariance.

Target missed

The design target was aspirational, not measured

The hardening note proposed ≥95% recall at ≤5% budget. The measured value is 37.1%. Routing genuinely works — 7.4× better than random — but that headline is not supported by this run and should not appear on a slide.

Holds, with a caveat

The mechanism survives a new network. The calibration does not.

Trained on a dev-heavy organisation and deployed cold to an OT-heavy one, detection quality barely moves. But the alert threshold set at the training world's 99th percentile fires on 6.3% of windows there — and 6.8% in its own world's test period. So it isn't a shift problem, it's a temporal one: the threshold decays from the day it is set.

Recall vs escalation budget

The budget is the share of host-windows handed up to the expensive Inspector. Recall is the share of windows the Inspector would have flagged that the router actually sent it. The diagonal is what you'd get by escalating at random. Drag the scrubber, or hover the plot.

A is the original V2 design: the Sentry is a shrunk detector. B distils only the encoder and scores each window against that host's own baseline. C replaces the threshold with a learned deferral rule. The Inspector itself scores AUC against attack windows, so it is a real oracle — agreement with it is not agreement with noise.

Does the Sentry preserve the Inspector's geometry?

The Mahalanobis approach only means something if the small encoder still places each host's neighbours where the large one did. The honest test is neighbourhood overlap, not score correlation — score correlation stays high while the local structure quietly collapses.

Does it survive a network it has never seen?

The encoder trains on world A — a dev-heavy software org — then deploys cold to world B: OT/plant-heavy, different role mix, slower human rhythms, lower data volumes, longer working day. Baselines are re-fit on B's own commissioning window, but A's threshold and A's standardiser are applied unchanged, because that is the mistake a real deployment makes.

The mechanism transfers. The calibration does not. Detection quality barely moves across a genuinely different organisation. But the threshold set at world A's 99th percentile fires on of world B — and on of world A's own test period. So this is not a shift problem, it is a temporal one: reconstruction error in the test period runs systematically higher than in the commissioning period even in the same world. The threshold decays from the day it is set.

Consequence: every deployment needs its own calibration window and rolling re-calibration. The current design has no such knob.

How it works

Two pictures. The first is the system: who looks at what, and when. The second is what the trial actually varied — because all three routers share the same small encoder and differ only in the box that makes the call.

The words, plainly:

  • Inspector / teacher — the big model. Sees each host in the context of every other host. Accurate, needs a GPU, too expensive to leave running.
  • Sentry / student — the small model that runs all the time. Its job is not to catch attacks. Its job is to decide what deserves the Inspector's time.
  • Encoder, "the eyes" — the part that turns an hour of a host's traffic into 32 numbers. Compressing this survives; compressing the judgement does not.
  • Baseline — what this host's 32 numbers usually look like, stored once during commissioning. Three clusters per host, so a night shift isn't mistaken for an intruder.
  • Escalation budget — the share of windows you're willing to send back up. Set it by what you can afford; the chart tells you what that buys.

Before quoting any of this

The attack numbers come from a stand-in, not a dataset

A detector evaluated on the generator that produced its attacks learns the generator. The benign side is synthetic too, and unrealistic benign data is the more damaging error — it makes every false-positive figure meaningless. Replace it with real consented capture before any external claim.

What the comparison above does not depend on is whether the attacks are realistic: the Inspector is the reference oracle, so router-versus-teacher agreement is measured against a fixed target either way. That is the part you can defend. The generator does carry the hard negatives that matter — DevOps hosts producing the same Recon → Repo → Messaging → Cloud category sequence as the attack, with human timing.

The claim this run supports
At a 5% escalation budget the Sentry recovers 37% of everything the Inspector would have flagged — 7.4× better than random routing — while being 13.7× smaller and running on CPU. Detection quality holds on a different organisation; the alert threshold does not, and must be recalibrated per deployment and over time.

Still not built:

  • No live escalation loop — B is a scorer, not yet the architecture. No retention rule, no random-sampling scheduler, no re-baselining, no round-trip to the Inspector.
  • The Inspector is a reimplementation of GraphIDS (arXiv:2509.16625). Cite it; it is not the contribution.
  • Hop-2 context uses the full same-category population, not a sampled fanout. Fine at this scale; must become neighbour sampling on real graphs.
  • Mahalanobis wins here despite the documented near-OOD weakness in high dimensions. That makes the threshold empirical, not the objection wrong.
